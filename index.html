Associate Research Fellow
Korea Institute for Industrial Economics & Trade (KIET)

I am an economist at the Korea Institute for Industrial Economics & Trade (KIET). My research focuses on macroeconomics, with particular interests in firm dynamics and productivity, housing markets, and household finance. I study how firms and households respond to economic shocks and policy changes through employment, investment, housing, and borrowing decisions.

Research interests: Macroeconomics; Firm Dynamics and Productivity; Housing Markets and Household Finance

Research

Publication

Employment, Productivity, and Business Groups: Evidence from Chaebols in Korea

Taehoon Kim

International Journal of the Economics of Business, 32(2), 197–227, 2025

Abstract

Using confidential Korean firm-level data, I examine how business-group ownership shapes firms' employment decisions. Chaebol affiliates show employment-adjustment patterns distinct from those of unaffiliated firms. I document spillovers among firms within the same group and differences in affiliates' roles: central firms appear to absorb labor-market adjustments associated with shocks to other group members. I then develop a model of business groups to explain these patterns. When firing costs apply at the group level, the model generates sluggish employment responses and labor hoarding by central firms. The findings suggest that policies intended to protect jobs may impose substantial costs on economy-wide efficiency when firms coordinate employment within business groups.

Working Papers

Working Paper · August 2026

Declining Business Dynamism in Korea: Shocks, Responsiveness, and the Role of Mature Firms

with Yoonsoo Lee and Jungmin Han

Abstract

Business dynamism has declined sharply in Korea: job reallocation, entry, and exit have all fallen by roughly half since the early 2000s. Following Decker et al. (2020), we ask whether businesses face smaller idiosyncratic shocks or respond less to them. Productivity dispersion did not systematically decline, innovation dispersion fell only modestly, and measured persistence rose, offering at most limited support for a shock-based explanation. Because the standard growth measure embeds exit, we separate employment adjustment from survival. Employment adjustment weakens most among young establishments, as in the United States; survival selection, by contrast, deteriorates among mature establishments, a pattern that survives a fixed-cohort test. Restoring both margins to their 2000s responsiveness recovers about three-fifths of the reallocation decline and nearly one percentage point of annual labor productivity growth.

Working Paper · Preliminary and incomplete draft (October 29, 2026)

What Happens When Mortgage Credit Stops at a Price Threshold? Housing Transactions, Reallocation, and Welfare in Korea

Taehoon Kim

Abstract

This paper studies how price-threshold mortgage restrictions affect housing market activity, housing allocation, and welfare. Focusing on Korea’s December 2019 ban on purchase mortgages for apartments above KRW 1.5 billion, I document sharp bunching below the threshold and a significant decline in sales of exposed apartments, while relative prices respond more moderately. Motivated by these patterns, I develop a heterogeneous-household model with indivisible housing and borrowing constraints. In the long run, the restriction lowers the house price by 3% and aggregate debt by 10%, while stationary welfare rises by 0.47%, driven by changes in the household distribution. In partial equilibrium, holding the house price fixed at its pre-reform level, the restriction induces households alive at the reform to move down the housing ladder. However, in general equilibrium, the house price falls by 7.7% on impact, immediately reversing most of this downward reallocation. Yet this price decline amplifies welfare losses for incumbent households from 0.05% to 0.26% of consumption, mainly through lower collateral values that spread the losses beyond the targeted segment, especially to low-income households. Finally, among policies achieving the same long-run debt reduction, I find that a uniform loan-to-value (LTV) cut imposes the largest welfare loss, whereas loan caps that tighten borrowing limits more gradually generate the smallest losses. These findings highlight that welfare costs depend not only on which purchases are restricted but also on how borrowing limits are designed.
